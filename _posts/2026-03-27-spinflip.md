The hydrogen line is a radio signal at 1420,405 MHz emitted by neutral hydrogen atoms throughout the galaxy. It is one of the most important signals in radio astronomy — its frequency is universal, and mapping its Doppler shift across the sky reveals the structure and rotation of the Milky Way. It can also be detected with relatively simple hardware, which is why it is a popular target for amateur radio astronomers.

Spinflip is my attempt at building such a telescope, inspired by [CHART](https://github.com/astrochart/CHART) — the Completely Hackable Amateur Radio Telescope project. The name comes from the physics: the signal is produced when a hydrogen atom undergoes a spin-flip transition, where the electron and proton go from having parallel spins (higher energy) to antiparallel spins (lower energy), releasing a photon at exactly 1420,405 MHz. In a single atom this transition is incredibly rare — on average it takes about 10 million years — but the galaxy contains so much hydrogen that the cumulative signal is easily detectable.

## Hardware

The telescope is built around three components:

**Antenna** — a cardboard horn antenna (90 × 70 cm) built from scratch. The horn shape concentrates the incoming signal and rejects noise from wide angles. Cardboard is covered with aluminium tape to make it conductive.

**LNA** — a SAWbird+ H1 low-noise amplifier centred on 1420,405 MHz, providing around 40 dB of amplification. This is essential: the hydrogen line signal is extremely faint, and without amplification the noise floor of the SDR dongle would completely overwhelm it.

**SDR dongle** — an RTL-SDR Blog V4, a €40 USB device that receives radio signals between roughly 500 kHz and 1766 MHz. It digitises the signal and streams IQ samples to the computer over USB.

## Software

The software is written in Python and is available on GitHub: [nekhebit/spinflip](https://github.com/nekhebit/spinflip).

The signal processing pipeline works as follows:

1. The dongle is tuned 1 MHz above the hydrogen line (to 1421,405 MHz) so the line appears as an offset peak in the spectrum rather than at DC zero, which RTL-SDR hardware cannot accurately represent.
2. 100 blocks of 262 144 IQ samples each are captured. For each block, an FFT is computed to get the power spectrum.
3. The 100 spectra are averaged together — this is called spectral averaging and it reduces broadband noise while preserving the real signal, which stays consistent across captures.
4. The result is displayed with frequency in MHz on the x-axis and power in dB on the y-axis.

The project includes a command-line observation script, a graphical interface for setting parameters, and a standalone FITS viewer for reviewing saved observations.
